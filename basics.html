No physics background needed · Five quick steps

Quantum computing, in five clicks

Before you watch a quantum code catch errors, it helps to know what a qubit actually is and why protecting one is so hard. No equations here, just buttons to press. Each step takes about a minute.

Step 1 of 5

What's a qubit?

An ordinary bit is either 0 or 1. A qubit can be 0, or 1, or a blend of both at once. Click the three states below and watch the arrow swing. The arrow pointing up means "definitely 0," down means "definitely 1," and sideways means "an even blend."

|0⟩, definitely 0
A qubit at |0⟩: if you measured it now, you'd get 0 every time.
Takeaway: a qubit isn't limited to 0 or 1. It can hold a blend of both, and that's where all of quantum computing's extra power comes from.
Step 2 of 5

The catch: measuring forces a choice

Here's the strange part. While a qubit is in a blend, it really is "both." But the instant you measure it, it snaps to a single answer, 0 or 1, chosen at random, weighted by the blend. You can't peek without disturbing it. Put this qubit in an even blend, then hit Measure over and over and watch the tally.

Even blend
00
10
An even blend should land on 0 and 1 about half the time each.
Takeaway: you can't just read a qubit to check it. Measuring collapses the blend and destroys the information. This is the whole reason error correction is hard, and the clever trick of the surface code is checking for errors without measuring the data.
Step 3 of 5

What's an "error," then?

Noise nudges a qubit in one of two basic ways. An X flip swaps 0 and 1 (a "bit-flip"). A Z flip leaves 0 and 1 alone but flips the sign of the blend (a "phase-flip"), which is invisible to a simple 0/1 reading but very real. Click each flip and watch what changes.

|0⟩
Start at |0⟩. Try an X flip, then a Z flip.
Takeaway: these two flips, X and Z, are the exact errors you'll inject in the playground. Every error a real qubit suffers is some combination of them.
Step 4 of 5

Why not just keep a backup copy?

With ordinary bits, you'd guard against errors by copying: store three copies, and if one flips, the other two outvote it. The obvious fix. But quantum physics flatly forbids it: there is no operation that copies an unknown qubit. It's called the no-cloning theorem. Try it.

Press the button to attempt a copy.
Takeaway: no backups allowed. So instead of copying one qubit, error correction spreads a single piece of information across many qubits in a pattern, so a few failures can be detected and undone. That pattern is exactly what the surface code is.

Now you know what an error is.

You've met the qubit, seen why you can't just read or copy it, and learned the two kinds of error: X and Z. That's everything you need to watch a real quantum code catch one in the act.
